\abstract{
Knowledge of the outermost layer of Earth's atmosphere, known as the exosphere, is important for understanding processes like plasmaspheric refilling and ring current recovery after geomagnetic storms.
The Carruthers Geocorona Observatory, launched in September 2025, represents the first mission dedicated to studying this region of our planet's atmosphere with continuous, wide-field observations of scattered Lyman-$\alpha$ H emission from a distant vantage at the Earth-Sun L1 Lagrange point.  By iteratively fitting a model to these detected Lyman-$\alpha$ emissions, it is possible to computationally recover a plausible global H density distribution through tomographic retrieval.
In this work, we present a flexible 3D parametric retrieval model which improves upon previous approaches by replacing rigid radial density decay assumptions and density priors with a truncated spherical harmonic basis and adaptable cubic b-splines, performing strongly in geomagnetically quiet conditions.




}

\section{Introduction}\label{intro}

The Carruthers Geocorona Observatory is NASA's first mission dedicated to investigating the fundamental nature of Earth's exosphere. The Carruthers mission is designed to provide novel measurements of exospheric Hydrogen (H) emission at 121.6 nm (Lyman-alpha, Ly-$\alpha$) from an ideal vantage with a spatial resolution and temporal cadence unmatched by any previous platform.  From its halo orbit around the Earth-Sun L1 Lagrange point, Carruthers will have an unobstructed Earth-nadir view and full control over its pointing, enabling nearly continuous, high cadence imaging of exospheric H emission as well as off-nadir imaging in support of image calibration. 

Knowledge of the structure of the Earth's exosphere, the outermost layer of atmosphere, is critical for understanding several important atmospheric processes, including energy dissipation and plasma transport following geomagnetic storms \cite{ilie2013} \cite{liu2022}. Additionally, the exosphere serves as the pathway for permanent escape to space of H atoms, the dominant constituent of the exosphere and a key driver of long-term surface water loss on Earth.

  \label{fig:previous_mission}

While direct, in-situ measurement is possible with mass spectrometers, the sheer size of the exosphere makes this approach impractical.  Ground-based remote sensing has been hampered by the fact that thermospheric O\textsubscript{2} is opaque to UV and by limited measurement angles due to a fixed sensor platform.
  However, attempts have been made to exploit vantage evolution of rotating Earth's surface \cite{balmer2}.
  Visible H emission (Balmer-$\alpha$ line) from resonant fluorescence has been observed on the ground for decades \cite{balmer1}, but the signal is very dim and no reliable density distributions have been derived due to limited data constraints from ground-based sensors.  To date, no reliable retrievals have been derived from these measurements.

  Lyman-$\alpha$ is ideal as a target to study the exosphere, as these are photons excited by resonant scattering from the ground state and produce very bright emissions.
  Lyman-$\alpha$ studies of the exosphere have occurred sporadically for decades, but usually as opportunistic measurements or as a secondary science goal.
 Figure \ref{fig:previous_mission} provides an overview of key historical observations with accompanying descriptions given below.

  An early exospheric measurement came from OGO-5 in 1969 which detected H atoms out to 7 Re and proved the existence of a geotail (a Sun-Earth aligned protrusion) and Lyman-$\alpha$ background from (IPH) \cite{ogo5} \cite{baliukin}.
  This was followed by Apollo 16 in 1972 which carried a photocathode-based UV imager to the Lunar surface that serves as the basis for modern UV cameras. The Apollo 16 experiment obtained a single image, the first ever wide-field exospheric measurement \cite{apollo2} \cite{apollo}.
  Much later Galileo performed an Earth flyby during a gravity assist in 1991 towards Jupiter, capturing a single composite image which indicated an exospheric radial extent beyond that found by Apollo.
  A few years later in 1996, the SOHO spacecraft with its SWAN hydrogen absorption cell \cite{soho_swan} launched towards Lagrange point L1.  SWAN captured full-sky Lyman-$\alpha$ images, with some high-resolution scans directed towards the Earth, detecting the presence of exospheric H atoms out to 100 Re \cite{baliukin}.
  From 2000 to 2005, the IMAGE spacecraft and GEO instrument with three scanning photometers slowly scanned over different altitudes, detecting the presence of a geotail.  The spacecraft additionally found significant temporal variation in measured flux, which was not well explained by solar variation, suggesting complicated exosphere dynamics \cite{ostgaard}.
  From 2001 to 2025, the TIMED spacecraft and GUVI instrument launched and began long-term UV imaging through the limb near the exobase, finding an inverse correlation between H density at the exobase and solar activity \cite{timed_guvi2}.
  In 2015, the PROCYON spacecraft and LAICA instrument captured a single wide-field from 2350 Re, with studies reporting agreement between the retrieved H density distribution and predictions from radiation pressure theory \cite{procyon_laica}.
  In the same year, the dual TWINS spacecraft launched and began acquiring composite Lyman-$\alpha$ measurements, identifying storm-time variations in the exosphere with radial enhancements of H density propagating outwards \cite{gonzalo_storm} \cite{zoennchen2011}.


  Of these missions, only TWINS and TIMED have observed the exosphere over multi-year timescales, but their low-altitude observations and measurement gaps limit their utility in understanding global exospheric dynamics.
  Long-term study of the exosphere has not been a priority on any mission, and only 4 wide-field images have ever been acquired \cite{apollo2} \cite{galileo} \cite{procyon_laica2} \cite{ogo5}.


By mathematically modelling the propagation of solar photons through the exosphere, a 3D reconstruction of the exosphere which is consistent with long-baseline, wide-FOV 2D images produced by the Carruthers cameras can be obtained through a tomographic retrieval process.
This paper focuses on the region of the exosphere where H density is low enough that most solar photons scatter once before entering the detector, known as the optically thin regime, but details of the retrieval process for the optically thick regime can be found in (FIXME: citation).

The rest of this paper will describe the emission model and acquisition process of Carruthers measurements, processing of raw sensor data to useful scientific constraints, details of the tomographic inverse problem, a new generalized low-parameter model for representing exospheric H density, and finally validation experiments against realistic ground truths with measurement noise representative of the Carruthers cameras.

The next section will describe the Carruthers orbit, camera geometry, physics of Lyman-$\alpha$ photon propagation through an optically thin exosphere and an overview of the instrument model.

%% =============== EMISSION MODEL =================


In order to recover a hydrogen density distribution from measurements, it is necessary to mathematically model how Lyman-$\alpha$ photons propagate through the exosphere and enter the camera.
This is known as an emission model and is a central component of tomographic retrieval algorithms.

  \st{Numerically modeling the physics of radiative transfer is a computationally complex task, as solar Lyman-$\alpha$ photons entering the atmosphere usually scatter multiple times, creating nonlinear interdependencies between distant portions of the exosphere.  However,} In regions of the atmosphere where hydrogen is sparse (known as the optically thin regime) it is a valid approximation that incident solar photons resonantly scatter only once \cite{ostgaard}, simplifying computational requirements and implementation complexity of the emission model.
  Anderson and Hord Jr. \cite{opticaldepththin} define the optically thin regime as regions where optical depth $\tau <= 0.1$.

  With these assumptions, the measurement by the spacecraft is proportional to a line integral of the total mass of hydrogen present in the atmosphere along the LOS of a particular pixel, given by photon radiance

  \begin{equation}
    I_{\text{exo},t} = \frac{g_t}{4 \pi} \phi (\beta)
    \int_S \bm{a}_t (\vc{s}) \bm{\rho}_t (\vc{s}) \dif s
    \text{  (phot/s/cm$^2$/sr) }
    \label{eqn:emission_model}
  \end{equation}

    %% #gt([(phot/s/cm$^2$/sr)])

  where $S$ is the pixel line-of-sight (LOS), $g_t$ is solar g factor, $t$ is time, $\phi$ is the scattering phase function, $\bm{a}_t$ is albedo correction factor, $\vc{s}$ is position vector and $\bm{\rho}$ is hydrogen density, illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:emission_model_physics}.
  Table \ref{tab:emission_units} gives an overview of different measurement quantities and their units.

  $I_{\text{exo}_t}$ is line-integrated radiance, an integral over wavelength of the Lyman-$\alpha$ emission line whose width is due to thermal broadening.

The scattering phase function $\phi$ represents the anisotropic directional distribution of resonantly-scattered photons relative to the direction of the Sun \cite{chamberlain}.

The solar g factor $g_t$ (phot/s/atom) relates hydrogen density (atoms/cm$^3$) to photon flux emitted per unit volume (phot/s/cm$^3$) \cite{gfactor}.   The quantity $g_t / 4 \pi \cdot \phi(\beta)$ describes the rate of photons being emitted into a part of the sky (1 steradian) from a unit volume of gas and is assumed to be a known, external input to the emission model.  The solar g factor is proportional to incident solar Lyman-$\alpha$ flux at line center, which is time-varying and measured on-orbit.

    Finally, $\bm{a}_t (\vc{s})$ is a unitless multiplicative correction factor (assumed known) to account for high-density regions of the inner optically thick exosphere which act as a secondary source of Lyman-$\alpha$ photons illuminating the outer exosphere from below.

\st{(FIXME: delete this paragraph?) The scope of this manuscript does not include these correction terms but may be included in a future work.}

A tenuous distribution of hydrogen throughout the solar system, known as interplanetary hydrogen (IPH), also contributes to the line-integrated Lyman-$\alpha$ radiance detected by the spacecraft.
  IPH, like solar g factor, is measured independently by Carruthers (FIXME: cite Alex?) and is assumed to be known.
  The impact of biases introduced by IPH subtraction has been considered to make sure retrievals still meet error requirements.

Together, these radiance sources are referred to as $I_\text{bkg}$.

\section{Discretization and Inverse Problem}\label{sec:inverse_problem}

An inverse problem is the procedure of determining the causative factors of a set of measurements derived from an observation process.  In exospheric tomography, the factor driving the magnitude of column density measurements is the density distribution of hydrogen in the regions being observed.  \st{Figure \ref{fig:inverse_problem_schematic} shows a generalized example of an inverse problem}

%% \begin{figure}[h]
%%   \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/inverse_problem}
%%   \caption{General schematic for an observing system and inverse problem}
%%   \label{fig:inverse_problem_schematic}
%% \end{figure}

\st{where $\bm{\rho}$ and $\bm{\hat{\rho}}$ are the ground truth and estimated density distribution, $I$ and $C$ are instrument model and calibration, $\bm{y}$ are column density measurements, $\epsilon$ is measurement noise,  $R$ is a retrieval algorithm, and $F_0$ is a forward tomographic operator describing reality while $F$ is a numerical approximation.  This distinction between forward operators matters when considering model uncertainty or mismatch discussed in (FIXME: remove this reference, will not include in paper).}

In general, direct analytic solutions to tomographic inverse problems are infeasible, necessitating a numerical approach where the forward operator is discretized and the solution space is divided into a finite grid of non-overlapping regions called voxels where density is assumed to be constant.


\st{There are a large variety of grid types to choose from, including regular grids (e.g. spherical, cylindrical, cartesian), non-regular grids (HEALPIX \cite{healpix}, cubed sphere), adaptive grids \cite{adaptivemesh1}, or grids which may utilize hierarchical structures (octree) and tetrahedral meshes.

Choosing a discretization grid which is well-suited to the data is critical, as an improper grid can create aliasing and other artifacts that cause the numerical result to deviate from the functions they approximate.  Since the exosphere is well-understood to smoothly vary with larger density gradients at lower altitudes, a regular spherical grid with logarithmically spaced radial bins is appropriate.  As opposed to more sophisticated gridding schemes, regular grids allows for the use of a simple multidimensional array as the underlying data structure, and the property of shared boundaries between voxels simplifies tomography calculations, as demonstrated in Tomosphero (FIXME: introduce tomosphero earlier) \cite{tomosphero} (FIXME: tomosphero citation).}

    The regular spherical grid used in this manuscript has its 0$^\circ$ elevation pole aligned to ecliptic north (+Z Cartesian GSE axis) and $\pm$180$^\circ$ azimuth branch point pointed away from the Sun (-X Cartesian GSE axis), as shown in Figure \ref{fig:grid_details}.

    This manuscript uses notation convention $r$, $e$, $a$ when referring to radial, elevational, and azimuthal dimensions to avoid ambiguity with astrophysical and mathematical conventions for spherical coordinates that denote the elevational and azimuthal dimensions differently.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.4\linewidth]{figures/sph_convention}
  \caption{Spherical coordinates definition in GSE}
  \label{fig:grid_details}
\end{figure}

The grid resolution necessary to avoid aliasing and sample errors is data-dependent and was selected by exhaustive search to keep introduced error small while minimizing total number of voxels.

Under the conditions described in Section \ref{sec:measurement_constraints} (single scattering in an optically-thin exosphere) and ignoring noise, tomographic inversion can be formulated as the solution of the linear inverse problem

$$\bm{y} = F \bm{\rho}$$

with measurements $\bm{y}$, forward operator $F$, and density $\bm{\rho}$.

Direct inversion of the tomographic operator requires that the matrix $F$ be non-singular in order for an inverse $F^{-1}$ to uniquely exist.  However, tomography problems generally have fewer constraining LOS than free variables (voxels), making them ill-posed (lacking existence, uniqueness or stability of solutions).

While direct inversion of $F$ is impossible, the retrieval problem $R$ of obtaining estimate $\hat{\bm{\rho}}$ can be reformulated as a minimization of the objective function

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\bm{\rho}} = R(\bm{y}) = \min_{\bm{\rho}} || \bm{y} - F M(\bm{c}) ||_2^2 + \mathcal{R}_1(\bm{c}, \bm{\rho}) + ...
\label{eqn:cost_function}
\end{equation}

where $M$ is a parametric representation of density $\bm{\rho}$ (referred to as a model in this manuscript), and $\mathcal{R}$ is a regularization term.

%% Together, the model and regularizers serve to , especially in the presence of noise where small perturbations in the measurements $\bm{y}$ are amplified greatly in the inversion process.

\st{A generalized inverse such as the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse, can be constructed from $F$ which selects the solution $\bm{\rho}$ with the smallest norm or which best fits the measurements.  Another approach is to assume that $\bm{\rho}$ has some low degree-of-freedom representation on a subspace (linear) or manifold (non-linear) within the space of all solutions.}



%% If the model is sufficiently low-dimensional and linear, then uniqueness is guaranteed if a solution exists.

The model serves to reduce the number of plausible solutions by assuming that $\bm{\rho}$ has some low degree-of-freedom representation $\bm{c}$ on a subspace (linear) or manifold (non-linear) within the space of all solutions.

However, this solution may still be ill-conditioned, or unstable in the presence of noise where small perturbations in the measurements $\bm{y}$ are amplified greatly in the inversion process.
    This is especially true of computational imaging problems like tomography, where smoothing effects of integration dampen the high frequency information about $\bm{\rho}$ available in the measurements.
The regularization terms in Equation \ref{eqn:cost_function} encode prior knowledge about $\bm{\rho}$ and $\bm{c}$ which may not be captured by the model and can be used to combat ill-posedness.

  \st{An alternative approach is to view inverse problems as a probabilistic inference problem which uses Bayes theorem to model uncertainty in reconstruction $\bm{\rho}$ given measurements $\bm{y}$.}

\st{The issues of solution existence, uniqueness and sensitivity in inverse problems were formally described by Hadamard in the early 20th century and used to define ill-posed and ill-conditioned inverse problems \cite{hadamard} \cite{illposed}.}

    The notation introduced in this section is summarized in Table \ref{tab:symbols} and will be used throughout the manuscript.

  \st{These principles of solution models and regularization have been used historically to guide retrieval algorithms and will be utilized

\section{Spherical Harmonic Spline Representation}
\label{sec:shsr}

Zoennchen et al. \cite{zoennchen_new} presented a retrieval method (derived from earlier work \cite{zoennchen2011}, \cite{zoennchen2013}, \cite{zoennchen_old}) featuring a partially separable parametric model based on a spherical harmonic representation (SHR), meaning the model can be expressed as a product of lower-dimensional functions.
Spherical harmonics are a family of complex, two-dimensional, continuous, orthonormal functions defined over a spherical domain.
\st{These functions are commonly used in planetary atmospheric sciences \cite{hodges}, atomic physics (electron orbitals), computer graphics and more.}
They serve as an analog to the Fourier basis in a spherical domain and approximate smoothly-varying spherical functions by linear combination of the basis.

%% // FIXME: ital degree order

Spherical harmonics are typically denoted $Y_{l m}(e, a)$ where $l$ and $m$ are known as the function degree and order.  In planetary physics, the convention is to denote coefficients of this linear combination as $A_{l m}$ (when $m \geq 0$) and $B_{l m}$ (when $m < 0$).  Figure \ref{fig:sph_harm} gives a graphical overview of the real/imaginary parts of the first few degrees of spherical harmonic functions.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/harm}
  \caption{First 3 degrees of spherical harmonic functions}
  \label{fig:sph_harm}
\end{figure}

The model density distribution assumed by Zoennchen et al. \cite{zoennchen_new} is given as

\begin{equation}
  n_H (r, e, a) = c r^{-k} d^{1/r} \text{SHR}(r, e, a)
  \label{eqn:radial_decay}
\end{equation}

\begin{align*}
  \text{SHR}(r, e, a) = \\
    \sqrt{4 \pi} \sum_{l=0}^3 \sum_{m=0}^l
     &A_{l m}(r) \cdot \text{Re}[Y_{l m}(e, a)] \\
    +& B_{l m}(r) \cdot \text{Imag}[Y_{l m}(e, a)]
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
  A_{l m}(r) &= a_{l m} + b_{l m} \ln{r} \\
  B_{l m}(r) &= p_{l m} + q_{l m} \ln{r}
\end{align*}

\st{Previous models are either incapable of representing spherical asymmetries, require a density prior (problematic for an understudied atmospheric regime), or lack the interpretability of a spherical harmonic basis.}

This section describes a new partially separable model which also uses spherical harmonics as basis functions \st{like the one used by Zoennchen et al}.
but replaces the relatively strong assumption of an inverse power law governing radial density variation with a more adaptable cubic spline model.
This formulation is less strict about the shape of radial density profiles but still enforces smoothness of spherical harmonic coefficients.

The density model is written as

\begin{align}
  M(\bm{c}) &:= H S \bm{c} = \sum_{l=0}^L \sum_{m=-l}^l H_{l m}(e, a) S_{l m}(r) \\
  S_{l m}(r) &= \sum_{k=1}^K c_{l m k} B_{k}(r)
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
  H_{l m}(e, a) =
  \begin{cases}
    \text{Re}[Y_{l m}(e, a)] \text{ if } m \geq 0 \\
    \text{Im}[Y_{l m}(e, a)] \text{ if  } m < 0 \\
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

  where $S_{l m}(r)$ is a 1D cubic spline function composed of $K$ third order B-splines $\{B_1, ..., B_K\}$ that define spherical harmonic coefficients between $K$ radially log-spaced control point shells, illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:sph_harm_spline} with truncation order $L=3$.  This basis function formulation for $H_{l m}$ is equivalent to the one presented above but avoids notational awkwardness when $m \geq 0$ versus $m < 0$.


\begin{figure}[h]
  \includegraphics[trim={0 0 12cm 0},clip,width=1\linewidth]{figures/harm_spline}
  \caption{Smooth variation of coefficients in radial direction enforced by B-splines}
  \label{fig:sph_harm_spline}
\end{figure}

The spherical harmonic functions are aligned to the GSE coordinate system, with +Z as the primary axis of symmetry and the positive bulge of $H_{1 1}$ oriented towards the Sun in the +X direction.

The number and location of the $K$ control points used in the spline functions and the maximum spherical harmonic degree $L$ are selected at model initialization time.

\st{(FIXME - remove this) static-justification justifies specific choices of $K$ and $L$ for the Carruthers mission.}

The model inputs are

$$\bm{c} = \{c_{l m k}, ... : l \leq L, |m| \leq l, k  \leq K \}$$

and the minimization problem is

\begin{align}
    \hat{\bm{c}} =& \arg \min_{\bm{c}} ||\bm{y} - FM(\bm{c})||_1^2 \notag \\
    &+ \lambda_1 ||S(\bm{c})||_1 + \lambda_2 ||\text{clip}_{(-\infty, 0)}(M(\bm{c}))||_1
    \label{eqn:sphharmspline_min}
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
  \bm{\hat{\rho}} = M(\bm{\hat{c}})
\end{equation}

    Equation \ref{eqn:sphharmspline_min} uses an $L_1$ fidelity loss for robustness against outliers in measurements $\bm{y}$, an $L_1$ regularization (LASSO) on spline-interpolated coefficients for model interpretability, and an $L_1$ penalization term on negative densities to enforce model correctness.
    Note that a choice of $L=0$ enforces spherical symmetry, which can be useful for single snapshot retrievals.

    Because of the generalizability of spline density profiles and interpretability of spherical harmonics by the atmospheric science community, this is the primary method that will be used by the Carruthers mission for retrieving exospheric density during exospheric quiet conditions.

\section{Validation Test Results}

%% - FIXME - cite preliminary thesis
%%   - inner mask radius
%%   cover letter - mention carruthers, exoplanetary studies, stars, planetary atmosphere, rt code
%%   APJ paper - mention onion peeling


The retrieval algorithm implicitly encodes assumptions about hydrogen density distribution (smoothness, spherical harmonic structure, etc.), and verification that these assumptions hold requires testing against plausible ground truth densities.  This section overviews some of the ground truth datasets that are available for validation, their origins, spatiotemporal coverage, and preliminary retrieval results.

    Using the parametric model described in \cite{zoennchen_new}, Zoennchen et all fit several density distributions to Lyman-$\alpha$ radiance measurements observed by TWINS from specific days in both solar minimum and maximum conditions during geomagnetic quiet conditions near Summer solstice.
    Notably, this dataset (referred to as the TWINS SPH dataset) was generated from the spherical harmonic model described in Section \ref{sec:shsr} with order L=3 and stronger assumptions about radial decay of hydrogen distribution.

  Another validation dataset which will be referred to as the VMB (Vidal-Madjar \& Bertaux) model \cite{vmb} is a physics-based simulation of the exosphere.  It takes as input 2D temperature and H density distributions at the exobase (derived from NRLMSIS 2.0 \cite{msis}) and propagates this H density upwards and laterally.  By choosing different dates of the MSIS inputs, the model supports generating both geomagnetically quiet and storm-condition densities.  This simulation utilizes Liouville's equation to propagate constraints from the exobase, ignoring physical processes which are known to influence exospheric hydrogen distribution at large radial distances, such as solar radiation pressure and charge-exchange.

  Table \ref{tab:datasets} gives an overview of these datasets.


  The Spherical Harmonic Spline method introduced in Section \ref{sec:shsr} will be used by the Carruthers mission for retrieval of the exospheric H density distribution for both geomagnetically active and quiet conditions.  During geomagnetically quiet conditions, the exosphere evolves slowly enough that the H density distribution may be considered static over a 2 week window of evolving vantages.  This window provides sufficient measurement diversity to capture small-scale structures within the density distribution, so Carruthers will use a spherical harmonic truncation order of L=3 during these periods.  All retrievals in this section use regularizer weights $\lambda_1=10^4$ and $\lambda_2=10$.

  Figures \ref{fig:recon2w_twins} and \ref{fig:recon2w_vmb} give accuracy error for a single static density retrieval over a 2 week observation window with evolving vantage on the TWINS SPH and VMB datasets.  The "true" coefficients are a direct least-squares fit of the Spherical Harmonic Spline model to the dataset 3D densities without forward model or noise.
  Notably, the retrieval error is the smallest in the transverse (YZ) plane which is especially noticeable in the VMB retrieval.  This plane is oriented roughly perpendicular to measured LOS, which intuitively should give good conditioning, and high retrieval quality to voxels in this region.  Vertical gray bars indicate the location of spline control points.

    Additionally, errors are larger in the low-altitude region near the midnight (-X) side of Earth, where no LOS intersect due to occlusion by the Earth and optically-thick exosphere.  Voxels in this region are constrained only by spatial smoothing in the reconstruction model.

In these periods, Carruthers will use an observation window of 6 hours and limit truncation order to L=0 (spherically symmetric) to reflect the fact that poor measurement diversity of this short window cannot capture significant non-radial spatial variation. \section{Conclusion}

The Carruthers Geocorona Observatory is the first NASA mission dedicated to study of the nature of Earth's exosphere.  By making long cadence observations from a distant vantage, the Carruthers mission will produce exospheric H density measurements with a spatiotemporal resolution that exceeds previous studies.  This paper describes an emission model and tomographic retrieval framework for obtaining 3D exospheric reconstructions in the optically-thin regime which are appropriate for both geomagnetically quiet and active conditions.  We introduce a new atmospheric model SHSP which utilizes spherical harmonics and splines to describe an exospheric H distribution with few parameters in a way that is both general and interpretable.  We have released an open source tomographic projector library TomoSphero which GPU-enabled and differentiable, enabling rapid development of and experimentation with new optically thin atmospheric models. Finally, we showed that reconstructions from this model meet mission requirements when evaluated against realistic ground truths with realistic view geometry and measurement noise.
